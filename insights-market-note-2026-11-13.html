Weekly Market Note

Stocks gain in a week that felt worse than it was

November 13, 2026 · Week of November 9 – 13

This was a week that felt worse than it was. The headlines were hard. Inflation came in hotter than expected, a Federal Reserve official raised the idea of a larger-than-usual rate increase, the 10-year Treasury yield had its largest weekly jump since April 2025, and the chief executive of NVIDIA resigned. Yet the S&P 500 finished 2.5% above where it ended the week before, and about four of every five stocks in the index rose. The gain was uneven. Nearly all of it came on Monday, chip stocks swung sharply from day to day, and bonds and gold lost ground.

Why the week felt rough

Most of the week’s gain came on Monday, when the S&P 500 rose more than 2%. After that, the index fell on three of the next four days and ended Friday about where it had closed on Monday. The Nasdaq Composite and smaller companies lost ground over those four days.

Stocks and bonds fell on the same days. Bonds often gain when stocks fall, which softens the loss for a household that owns both. This week they fell together on Tuesday, Thursday, and Friday. Thursday’s bond loss covers two days, because the bond market was closed on Wednesday, November 11 for Veterans Day. Add a run of difficult headlines, and the week felt worse than the final numbers show.

Stocks still finished ahead

For the full week, all four major U.S. stock indexes rose, and so did all 11 sectors of the S&P 500. Energy led, recovering part of the prior week’s drop even though oil prices slipped. The S&P 500 recovered most of the prior week’s 2.7% decline and ended about 3% below its October 6 record. A widely followed measure of how large investors expect stock swings to be, the VIX, also ended the week lower than it began.

The typical stock has done better than the index. The S&P 500 Equal Weight Index, which gives every company in the S&P 500 the same weight, rose 2.3% this week. Since the S&P 500’s record close on October 6, the equal-weight index has gained 4.0% while the S&P 500 has lost 3.3%. Technology, which carries the most weight in the S&P 500, has fallen about 14% over that time, and communication services about 11%. Health care and consumer staples have each gained more than 12% over the same period.

AI stocks bounced, but they have not recovered

Chip stocks had the widest swings of the week. An index of large chip companies rose nearly 7% on Monday and about 5.7% on Wednesday, then fell more than 3% on Friday. It finished the week higher, but it remains about 31% below the record it set in June.

The technology sector is still well below where it was a month ago. It rose 2.0% this week, less than seven of the other 10 sectors, and it is about 12% lower than it was a month ago. Communication services, which includes Alphabet and Meta Platforms, is down by about the same amount over the month.

NVIDIA added to the unease. On Thursday, November 12, the company announced that Jensen Huang had resigned as chief executive and as a member of its board, after weeks of news reports about his personal conduct and a review by the board. The chief financial officer, Colette Kress, is serving as interim chief executive while the board considers candidates from inside and outside the company. NVIDIA is a leading maker of the chips that run AI, and its shares fell nearly 13% for the week, while shares of some competing chip makers rose on hopes of winning business.

Other AI news was better. Anthropic updated its plans to sell shares to the public for the first time, and Google announced new systems built on its own AI chips. CoreWeave, which rents out computing power for AI, reported results ahead of expectations and raised its forecast, and Bloomberg reported that OpenAI brought in about $10 billion of revenue in a single quarter for the first time. On the other side, SoftBank, a Japanese company that invests heavily in AI, reported a large quarterly loss.

Inflation ran hotter than expected

Consumer prices rose 0.4% in October from September, more than the 0.3% economists had expected. Core prices, which leave out food and energy, rose 0.3% for a third straight month.

Part of today’s inflation comes from strong demand, not only from higher energy costs. That is LPL Research’s view of the report. Restaurant prices had their largest monthly increase of the year and airfares rose again, yet in LPL Research’s view demand for dining out and travel has held up. Prices for hospital services also rose quickly, which LPL Research ties to a shortage of qualified workers. One encouraging sign is rent, where inflation has stayed below 3% for the past 10 months.

A rate increase in December now looks likely. The Federal Reserve’s benchmark rate stands at 3.75% to 4.00%. Hours before the inflation report, Fed Governor Christopher Waller said an increase of half a point, twice the usual size, should be considered if core prices rose 0.3% or more, which they did. Two other Fed officials also said further increases may be needed. Based on the current data, LPL Research’s view is that a half-point increase is unlikely without a surprise in the data, and that a standard quarter-point increase at the December 8 and 9 meeting is more likely.

Bond yields jumped

The 10-year Treasury yield rose to 5.15% from 4.86%, its largest weekly increase since April 2025, and closed above 5% for the first time since late October. The 2-year yield, which follows expectations for the Fed most closely, rose to 4.88% from 4.51%. When yields rise, the prices of existing bonds fall. The Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Index, a broad measure of high-quality U.S. bonds, lost 1.3% through Friday afternoon, ending a run of five weekly gains.

The rise did not come from higher inflation expectations. Almost all of the increase in the 10-year yield came from the part of the yield that pays investors more than inflation. The inflation that bond investors expect over the next 10 years barely moved. That fits LPL Research’s view that investors were adjusting to a new question, which is how much the Fed raises rates, not when it does. Higher yields also mean that new money invested in bonds earns more income.

Markets abroad rose

In Europe, the STOXX 600, an index of 600 companies across Europe, rose 2.4%. It gained on all five days and reached its first record high since August. European markets hold fewer technology companies than the U.S. market, which has drawn investors looking beyond AI.

In Asia, Taiwan’s main index rose 10% and Japan’s Nikkei 225 rose 6.5%, one week after both had closed more than 20% below their records. Taiwan Semiconductor, the world’s largest contract chip maker, reported record sales for October. Stocks in Hong Kong and mainland China fell, partly on reports that the U.S. may further limit Chinese companies’ access to advanced chips through cloud providers.

Oil and gold fell

U.S. crude oil fell about 2.5% for the week, to about $89 a barrel. Prices dropped early in the week after President Trump said on Sunday, November 8, that new strikes on Iran were highly unlikely while talks continued. They rebounded on Thursday after he said the U.S. does not need a deal with Iran. Rising U.S. inventories, and a statement from the International Energy Agency that the oil market will have more supply than demand this quarter for the first time since the conflict began, limited the rebound. Even so, the energy sector of the S&P 500 is up about 45% this year, the most of any sector.

Gold fell about 4.6%. Higher bond yields and a stronger dollar make gold, which pays no interest, less attractive to hold.

The week ahead

Monday, November 16, brings a survey of manufacturers in New York State. On Tuesday, November 17, the government reports October retail sales and industrial production, Home Depot reports earnings before the market opens, and NVIDIA reports after the close. Wednesday, November 18, brings October housing starts, pending home sales, and the minutes of the Federal Reserve’s October meeting at 2 p.m. ET. Thursday, November 19, brings weekly jobless claims, a survey of manufacturers in the Philadelphia region, and earnings from Walmart. On Friday, November 20, the University of Michigan releases its final November survey of consumer sentiment.
