Weekly Market Note

Chip stocks and oil fall in the week of the midterm elections

November 6, 2026 · Week of November 2 – 6

The S&P 500 fell 2.7% this week, its largest weekly decline since April 2025. The Nasdaq Composite fell 3.4% and closed nearly 11% below its October 6 record. Chip makers fell sharply on Thursday as investors worried about how spending on artificial intelligence (AI) is being paid for and about new limits on data center construction after the November 3 midterm elections. Oil also fell on hopes for talks with Iran, and energy stocks fell with it. Outside those two groups, the market held up better. More than half of the companies in the S&P 500 rose for the week, and bonds gained for a fifth straight week as lower oil prices eased inflation worries and investors looked for safety late in the week, a move LPL Research calls a haven bid.

The election turned attention to data centers

Stocks were little changed through Tuesday. By early Wednesday, based on race calls by the Associated Press, Democrats had won control of the House and the Senate. Democrats also won the races for governor in Texas, Georgia, and Ohio. On Wednesday the S&P 500 rose 1.6%, and about three of every four stocks in the index rose as oil prices fell.

The mood changed on Thursday. Texas’s governor-elect, Gina Hinojosa, has pledged to stop approving new data centers until the state legislature passes new protections, adding to a partial pause on permits that the outgoing governor ordered in August. Investors worried that rules like these could slow construction of the data centers that run AI, and shares of some of the companies that supply them sold off. The S&P 500 fell 4.4%, its worst day since April 2025, and most stocks in the index fell with it. An index of large chip companies fell 12.2%. On Friday, chip makers recovered part of the loss.

Technology accounted for almost all of the decline. Technology companies make up nearly two-fifths of the S&P 500, and the sector fell 6.9%. Energy also fell 11.9% as oil prices dropped. However, five of the 11 sectors rose, led by consumer discretionary, up 2.7%, and communication services, up 2.3%.

Most stocks rose

The typical stock did better than the index. The S&P 500 Equal Weight Index, which gives every company in the S&P 500 the same weight, fell 1.1% this week, compared with 2.7% for the S&P 500. Slightly more than half of the stocks in the index rose. Since the S&P 500’s record close on October 6, the equal-weight index has gained 1.7% while the S&P 500 has lost 5.7%.

Markets abroad were mixed

Stock markets in Asia that make chips and other parts for AI data centers fell the most. Taiwan’s main index fell 12.2% and ended the week more than 20% below its October 6 record. Markets in South Korea and Japan also fell sharply. Markets less tied to chip making rose. Stocks in Hong Kong gained as investors favored consumer and internet companies there and in mainland China, and stocks in India rose as well. Australia and New Zealand also rose.

In Europe, the STOXX 600, an index of 600 companies across Europe, rose 0.2%. The Bank of England raised its main interest rate on Thursday, its first increase since August 2023, citing higher energy prices from the conflict in the Middle East.

Oil fell on hopes for talks with Iran

U.S. crude oil fell about 13% for the week, to about $91 a barrel, and Brent crude, the international benchmark, fell by about the same amount. Most of the drop came on Monday and Wednesday. President Trump told the Financial Times on Tuesday that the U.S. had no immediate plan to strike Iran, and on Wednesday Vice President JD Vance said the war was the reason Republicans lost, which raised hopes that the U.S. would step back. On Friday the Associated Press reported that Iran is reviewing proposals from Qatar about its nuclear program. The U.S. struck Iranian air defenses during the week, and attacks on ships near the Strait of Hormuz, along with tight diesel supplies, kept oil from falling further.

Supplies of diesel remain tight. U.S. inventories of distillate fuels, which include diesel and heating oil, fell in late October to their lowest level since May 2003, according to the Energy Information Administration. Even after the week’s drop, the energy sector of the S&P 500 was up 37.9% for the year through November 6, the most of any sector.

Bonds gained for a fifth week

The Bloomberg U.S. Aggregate Index returned 0.5%, including interest, through Friday afternoon, its fifth straight weekly gain, and the 10-year Treasury yield fell to 4.86% from 4.94%.

Lower inflation expectations drove this week’s drop in yields. As oil fell, investors expected less inflation ahead, which pulled yields down. Over the full year, though, inflation expectations have barely moved. Long-term rates are higher this year because investors want a better return on top of inflation, not because they expect more inflation.

Some AI-related borrowers came under strain. Several of CoreWeave’s bonds fell below 80 cents on the dollar during the week. Investors are separating AI companies with strong finances from those that depend on continued borrowing, which points to a problem for some companies rather than for the financial system.

Hiring was stronger than expected

Employers added 165,000 jobs in October, more than twice the roughly 60,000 that economists had expected, and job gains for August and September were revised higher. The unemployment rate fell, and the share of adults working or looking for work rose. Health care added 42,000 jobs, and management, scientific, and technical consulting services added about 17,000. Average hourly pay rose 3.1% from a year earlier, less than the rise in consumer prices over the year to September. One measure pointed the other way: a broader unemployment rate that also counts part-time workers who want more hours rose.

A rate increase in December is possible. The Federal Reserve, which raised rates in September, held its benchmark rate at 3.75% to 4.00% on October 28, with two voting members preferring another increase. Two other Fed officials said this week that they expect rates will need to go higher. Based on the current data, the job market gives the Fed room to raise rates at its December 8 and 9 meeting, with the October inflation report on November 10 the next test.

The week ahead

On Monday, November 9, the New York Fed releases its survey of consumer inflation expectations. The October consumer price index comes out on Tuesday, November 10, at 8:30 a.m. ET. Wednesday, November 11, is Veterans Day: stock markets are open, but the bond market is closed. Thursday, November 12, brings weekly jobless claims, existing home sales, and the federal budget for October. Friday, November 13, brings the October producer price index, which tracks the prices businesses receive.
